## 1. Patient Presentation and Clinical History

A 65-year-old right-hand-dominant man with a 10-year history of type 2 diabetes mellitus presented with progressive right shoulder pain and stiffness of approximately five months' duration.

His latest HbA1c was 8.3%, with diabetes management under the supervision of his diabetologist.

The onset was gradual and non-traumatic. The patient reported a persistent low-grade shoulder ache that increased during overhead reaching, dressing, particularly reaching behind the back, and lying on the affected side.

Night pain interrupted sleep approximately two to four times per night.

His symptoms affected:

- Dressing and personal care.
- Overhead reaching.
- Sleeping on the affected side.
- Desk-based professional activities.
- Light household activities.

There was no previous history of shoulder injury or surgery.

### Clinical timeline

- Approximately five months before baseline: Gradual onset of right shoulder pain and stiffness.
- Baseline: Initial clinical assessment and commencement of physiotherapy.
- Day 10: Completion of 10 supervised sessions.
- Week 6: Follow-up approximately four weeks after completion of supervised treatment.
- Month 3: Further clinical review.
- Month 6: Long-term functional outcome assessment.

## 2. Clinical Assessment and Findings

### Observation

- Mild forward-head posture.
- Rounded shoulders.
- Right scapula slightly elevated and protracted at rest.
- No gross swelling or visible muscle wasting.

|---|---:|

| Shoulder abduction | 70° |
| External rotation | 20° |
| Internal rotation | L5 |

### Passive Range of Motion (PROM)

| Movement | Baseline |
|---|---:|
| Shoulder flexion | 100° |
| Shoulder abduction | 80° |
| External rotation | 30° |
| Internal rotation | L4 |

A firm capsular end-feel was noted, with greater restriction in external rotation and abduction.

### Muscle Strength

Rotator cuff and deltoid isometric strength was approximately MRC grade 4-/5, with reduced performance secondary to pain inhibition.

### Special Tests and Screening

- Global shoulder movement restriction was consistent with adhesive capsulitis.
- Empty-can and lag signs were negative for a full-thickness rotator cuff tear.
- Cervical screening was clear.
- Neurological screening, including sensation, reflexes and myotomes, was intact.
- Skin sensation over the shoulder region was intact before heat application.
- No reported red-flag features such as fever, unexplained weight loss or progressive neurological deficit.

No imaging was performed.

### Clinical Impression

Right shoulder adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder), associated with type 2 diabetes mellitus, clinically corresponding to the late freezing / early frozen stage.

Significant rotator cuff tear, advanced glenohumeral osteoarthritis and cervical radiculopathy were considered clinically unlikely based on the presenting history and examination.

## 5. Physiotherapy Treatment Programme

**Treatment duration:** 10 consecutive days.

**Total supervised sessions:** 10.

The programme was individualised according to the patient's symptoms, movement restrictions and tolerance.

### Sessions 1–4: Pain Modulation and Gentle Mobility

Treatment included:

- Therapeutic ultrasound over the glenohumeral region using pulsed parameters within tolerance.
- Interferential therapy (IFT) over the painful shoulder region.
- Gentle grade I–II glenohumeral joint mobilisation.
- Distraction and inferior/posterior glides.
- Physiotherapist-applied posterior shoulder capsule stretching.
- Codman's pendulum exercises.
- Gentle shoulder pulley-assisted elevation.
- Moist heat after confirming intact skin sensation.
- Education regarding recovery expectations and activity modification.

### Sessions 5–8: Progressive Mobility and Early Motor Control

Treatment progression included:

- Progression of joint mobilisation according to irritability.
- Continued posterior capsule stretching.
- Light rotator cuff isometric exercises.
- Active-assisted shoulder range of motion.
- Shoulder pulley exercises.
- Scapular setting.
- Postural correction and movement education.

### Sessions 9–10: Strengthening and Functional Restoration

Treatment included:

- Theraband external rotation.
- Theraband internal rotation.
- Scaption within a comfortable range.
- Functional reaching exercises.
- Dressing-related functional practice.
- Home exercise programme finalisation.
- Ergonomic and activity-modification advice.

### Home Exercise Programme

The patient was instructed to perform a daily home programme consisting of:

1. Moist heat.
2. Codman's pendulum exercises.
3. Shoulder pulley exercises.
4. Posterior capsule stretching.
5. Light rotator cuff exercises.
6. Postural correction and holds.

Exercise dosage was progressed according to tolerance.

Adherence was reported as good during follow-up reviews.

No adverse events were reported during supervised treatment or the home programme.

Diabetes management, including HbA1c monitoring, remained under the care of the patient's diabetologist.

## 6. Clinical Progress and Follow-up

### Day 10: Immediate Treatment Outcome

After completing 10 consecutive supervised sessions:

- Activity/night pain decreased from 8/10 to 4/10.
- SPADI improved from 68 to 42.
- Active shoulder flexion increased from 90° to 125°.
- Active shoulder abduction increased from 70° to 105°.
- Dressing and light daily activities became easier.
- Sleep disturbance reduced.

### Week 6: Continued Functional Improvement

At approximately four weeks after supervised treatment completion:

- Activity/night pain was 3/10.
- SPADI improved to 34.
- Active shoulder flexion reached 138°.
- Active shoulder abduction reached 122°.

### Three-Month Follow-up

Further improvements were recorded:

At six months from baseline:

- Rest pain remained at 1/10.
- SPADI improved to 15.

- Active shoulder abduction reached 152°.
- Active external rotation reached 68°.

The patient reported functional independence during daily activities and desk-based work, with mild residual end-range stiffness, particularly in external rotation and full overhead elevation.

### Patient Perspective

## 7. Clinical Discussion and Evidence Context

Adhesive capsulitis is characterised by progressive shoulder pain and restriction of both active and passive glenohumeral movements.

The condition involves inflammatory changes followed by capsular fibrosis and contracture, producing characteristic movement restrictions, particularly in external rotation.

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is a recognised risk factor for adhesive capsulitis and may be associated with a more prolonged recovery.

Physiotherapy plays an important role in conservative management through:

- Pain management.
- Joint mobilisation.
- Progressive mobility exercises.
- Stretching.
- Rotator cuff strengthening.
- Scapular control.
- Functional rehabilitation.
- Patient education and home exercise adherence.

In this case, the treatment programme combined physical modalities, manual therapy, therapeutic exercises and structured self-management.

The patient's early improvement was demonstrated through reductions in pain and disability, accompanied by measurable gains in shoulder range of motion.

Further improvements were recorded throughout the six-month follow-up period.

Natural recovery is also an important consideration in adhesive capsulitis. The individual contribution of physiotherapy and natural recovery cannot be separated in a single-patient case report.

## 8. Clinical Limitations

- This is a single-patient clinical case report.
- No comparison group was included.
- Findings represent the clinical progress of this individual patient.
- The treatment programme involved 10 consecutive supervised sessions.
- Longer-term improvements were accompanied by continued home exercise adherence.
- No imaging was performed.
- Mild residual restriction remained at six months.

## 9. Conclusion

This case demonstrates substantial clinical and functional improvement following an individualised conservative physiotherapy programme for right-sided adhesive capsulitis associated with type 2 diabetes.

After 10 consecutive supervised physiotherapy sessions, activity/night pain decreased from 8/10 to 4/10, SPADI improved from 68 to 42, and active shoulder flexion increased from 90° to 125°.

At six months from baseline, the patient achieved further improvements, with activity/night pain reduced to 1/10, SPADI reaching 15 and active shoulder flexion improving to 158°.

The patient regained functional independence in daily activities, with only mild residual end-range stiffness.

This case highlights the importance of individualised physiotherapy assessment, progressive rehabilitation, structured home exercises and continued clinical follow-up in managing frozen shoulder.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**1. How long does frozen shoulder usually last?**

Frozen shoulder can progress over several months and may take 12–36 months or longer to resolve substantially. Recovery varies between individuals.

**2. Is daily physiotherapy necessary for frozen shoulder?**

Treatment frequency depends on pain, irritability, movement restrictions and individual rehabilitation requirements. Some patients benefit from more intensive supervised treatment, while others follow programmes with fewer supervised sessions and regular home exercises.

**3. Does diabetes affect frozen shoulder recovery?**

Diabetes is a recognised risk factor for frozen shoulder and may be associated with a longer recovery period. Diabetes management should remain coordinated with the treating physician.

**4. Will full shoulder movement return?**

Many patients achieve substantial improvements in shoulder mobility and function. Some may retain mild residual stiffness.

**5. When should I seek physiotherapy for frozen shoulder?**

A physiotherapy assessment is advisable when progressive shoulder stiffness, pain, night discomfort or difficulty performing daily activities develops.

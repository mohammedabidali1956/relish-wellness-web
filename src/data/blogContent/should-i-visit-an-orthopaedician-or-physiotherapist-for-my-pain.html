Understanding the Roles of an Orthopaedic Specialist and a Physiotherapist

Pain can arise for different reasons. It may follow an injury, develop gradually, affect joint movement or be associated with an underlying medical condition.

Understanding the role of each healthcare professional can help you choose an appropriate first step.

When Should You Consult an Orthopaedic Specialist?

An orthopaedic specialist assesses conditions affecting the musculoskeletal system, including bones, joints, ligaments, tendons and related structures.

An orthopaedic consultation may be particularly appropriate when you have:

  • A suspected fracture or significant injury.
  • Severe pain following a fall, accident or sports injury.
  • A visibly deformed joint or limb after an injury.
  • Significant joint swelling following trauma.
  • Persistent joint symptoms that need further medical investigation.
  • Symptoms that may require specialist diagnosis or consideration of a surgical procedure.
  • A previously diagnosed orthopaedic condition requiring specialist follow-up.

Depending on your symptoms and examination findings, the specialist may recommend investigations, medication, a procedure, surgery or a non-surgical management plan.

Not every musculoskeletal problem requires surgery. The appropriate approach depends on the diagnosis, severity of the condition, your overall health and your functional needs.

When Should You Consult a Physiotherapist?

A qualified physiotherapist assesses pain, movement, mobility, strength and difficulties with everyday activities.

A physiotherapy consultation may be a suitable starting point when you experience problems such as:

  • Neck pain affecting movement.
  • Lower back discomfort that makes sitting, standing or bending difficult.
  • Shoulder stiffness that affects dressing or reaching overhead.
  • Knee pain that interferes with walking or climbing stairs.
  • Heel or ankle discomfort affecting standing and walking.
  • Movement limitations following an injury, once urgent conditions have been appropriately assessed.
  • Functional difficulties associated with posture, work activities or reduced mobility.

The physiotherapist considers your symptoms, medical history, movement limitations and other assessment findings to determine whether physiotherapy is appropriate.

Depending on the condition, care may include therapeutic exercise, movement training, activity guidance, rehabilitation and other clinically appropriate physiotherapy interventions.

If your symptoms suggest that you need further medical assessment, the physiotherapist can guide you towards an appropriate healthcare professional or service.

Can Both Professionals Be Involved in Your Care?

Yes. Some conditions benefit from care involving more than one healthcare professional.

For example, an orthopaedic specialist may evaluate a significant knee injury and establish the diagnosis. Physiotherapy may then form part of the rehabilitation plan, depending on the injury, treatment and clinical recommendations.

Similarly, someone with persistent shoulder pain may need further medical evaluation to clarify the cause, alongside rehabilitation when appropriate.

The aim is to identify the cause of the problem, address the patient's needs and support safe recovery. The appropriate care pathway depends on the individual rather than a fixed rule that everyone must follow.

Physiotherapy Before and After Orthopaedic Surgery

Before some planned procedures, the orthopaedic team may recommend preoperative rehabilitation, sometimes called prehabilitation. For example, NICE recommends preoperative rehabilitation advice for people having a hip or knee replacement, including exercises to do before and after surgery. The appropriate preparation depends on the operation and the person's health.

After surgery, physiotherapy may form part of the recovery plan. The timing, movement restrictions, weight-bearing guidance and exercises depend on the procedure and individual circumstances. Follow the treating surgeon's instructions; NICE guidance recommends early rehabilitation after primary elective hip, knee or shoulder replacement.

When physiotherapy is part of surgical care, the physiotherapist should work within the treating team's precautions and adapt rehabilitation to the patient's progress.

Can You Visit a Physiotherapist Directly in India?

Yes. You can approach a qualified physiotherapist directly in India for assessment of a suitable condition. You do not necessarily need to consult an orthopaedic specialist before every physiotherapy appointment.

The World Physiotherapy India country profile identifies direct access to physiotherapy as permitted. It also identifies circumstances in which referral or coordination with the treating medical team may be needed, including certain hospital inpatient, post-surgical, post-fracture and neurological cases.

The suitability of direct access depends on the patient's presentation and healthcare setting.

For example, if your shoulder feels stiff and you are struggling to reach overhead, a physiotherapy assessment may be an appropriate starting point. If your knee hurts during everyday activities, a physiotherapist can assess your movement and functional limitations.

However, severe pain following an accident, a suspected fracture or symptoms suggesting a medical emergency require the appropriate level of medical care.

Direct access does not mean that every condition should be managed with physiotherapy alone. Assessment helps determine the next appropriate step.

How Do You Decide Where to Start?

Consider the nature of your symptoms and how they began.

1. Pain That Develops Gradually

If discomfort develops gradually and affects ordinary activities such as sitting, walking, bending or reaching, a physiotherapy assessment may be appropriate when there are no warning signs suggesting an urgent medical problem.

The assessment can help determine whether your symptoms are suitable for physiotherapy or whether further evaluation is needed.

2. Pain Following an Injury

The severity and circumstances of an injury matter.

A significant fall, road accident, suspected fracture, obvious deformity or inability to use the injured limb may require prompt medical assessment before routine rehabilitation.

Do not attempt to manage a potentially serious injury through exercises alone.

3. Pain That Persists or Keeps Returning

Pain that continues, worsens or repeatedly interferes with daily life deserves appropriate assessment.

The next step depends on the location of the pain, the symptoms associated with it, your medical history and any previous investigations or treatment.

A physiotherapist can assess suitable movement-related problems. Further medical evaluation may be needed when the findings suggest another underlying cause.

4. Pain Accompanied by Other Symptoms

Pain accompanied by fever, unexplained illness, new neurological symptoms or other concerning changes may require medical assessment.

The urgency depends on the specific symptoms and circumstances. If you experience emergency warning signs, seek emergency care rather than arranging a routine physiotherapy appointment.

Do You Need an X-Ray or MRI Before Physiotherapy?

Not necessarily. An X-ray, MRI or other investigation is not required for every episode of muscle, joint or back pain.

Whether imaging is appropriate depends on the clinical presentation and whether the findings could change the next steps in care.

An orthopaedic specialist or another appropriate medical professional may recommend investigations when there is a clinical reason to investigate a possible fracture, significant injury or another underlying condition.

A physiotherapist can assess suitable movement-related problems and consider whether further medical evaluation is indicated.

If you already have relevant reports, bring them to your consultation. These may include:

  • Previous X-ray or MRI reports.
  • Relevant blood investigation results, if available.
  • Existing prescriptions and medical consultation summaries.
  • Previous rehabilitation records, if applicable.

You do not need to arrange every possible investigation before a routine physiotherapy appointment.

If you have experienced a serious injury or symptoms requiring urgent assessment, do not delay seeking medical care while arranging tests or a routine appointment.

What Happens During a Physiotherapy Assessment?

A physiotherapy assessment aims to understand your symptoms, how they affect your daily activities and whether physiotherapy is an appropriate option.

Depending on your condition, the consultation may include:

  • Symptom history: When the pain started, where you feel it, what makes it better or worse, and whether it is changing.
  • Medical history: Relevant previous injuries, health conditions, treatments and investigations.
  • Movement assessment: Appropriate examination of joint movement, mobility and activities affected by your symptoms.
  • Strength and function: Assessment of relevant strength, movement control and functional limitations.
  • Clinical reasoning: Consideration of the findings to determine whether physiotherapy is appropriate or whether further medical assessment is indicated.

Not every patient requires the same assessment procedures. The approach should be tailored to the individual's presentation.

The findings help guide the next steps, which may include physiotherapy, advice about activity, further assessment or referral where appropriate.

When Should You Seek Urgent Medical Attention?

Some symptoms should not wait for a routine physiotherapy appointment.

Seek prompt medical assessment for concerning symptoms such as severe or rapidly worsening pain, fever accompanied by significant pain, a substantial injury or unexplained deterioration in your health.

Seek emergency medical care if you experience warning signs such as:

  • New loss of bladder or bowel control associated with back pain.
  • New numbness around the groin or saddle area, particularly with back pain.
  • Sudden significant weakness or other sudden neurological symptoms.
  • Chest pain or severe difficulty breathing.
  • Sudden facial drooping, arm weakness or difficulty speaking, which may indicate a stroke.
  • A serious injury with an obvious deformity or suspected fracture.

This is not a complete list of emergencies. The seriousness of the symptoms and the circumstances in which they occur matter.

If you are uncertain about the urgency of your symptoms, seek appropriate medical advice. Do not delay emergency assessment to arrange routine physiotherapy.

What Should You Bring to Your First Appointment?

You can help make your consultation more useful by bringing relevant information about your symptoms and previous care.

Consider bringing:

  • Previous X-ray, MRI or other relevant investigation reports, if available.
  • Relevant blood investigation reports, if applicable.
  • Existing prescriptions and medical consultation summaries.
  • A list of current medicines, if relevant.
  • Details about when your symptoms started and which activities affect them.
  • Information about previous injuries, treatments or rehabilitation, where relevant.

You do not need to obtain investigations solely to attend a physiotherapy consultation unless there is a specific clinical reason to do so.

The assessment and next steps will depend on your symptoms, their severity, your medical history and the clinical findings.
